Delta did its takeoff math by hand on Sunday after its weight system failed

An automation failure at the airline's Atlanta hub forced manual weight-and-balance calculations, delaying more than half of Delta's flights days before Thanksgiving.

By Wire Willow Staff · Nov 22, 2026 · 2 min read

The story in 30 seconds

  • The failure: Delta's ALIS weight-and-balance system failed on Sunday, forcing crews to calculate takeoff weights by hand from the Atlanta operations center.
  • The scale: About 5,000 flights were affected; more than half were delayed and 6 percent canceled by 9:30 p.m. ET, per FlightAware, with more than 1,100 delays in Atlanta alone.
  • The response: The FAA briefly stopped Delta flights bound for Atlanta; automation was back by about 3:30 p.m., and 70 percent of the day's schedule changes came from the automation failure.
  • The context: Delta expects more than 4 million passengers through Atlanta between November 19 and December 1, its busiest Thanksgiving stretch.

Delta Air Lines spent Sunday doing by hand a calculation its computers normally handle in seconds. The airline's automated system for working out each departing plane's weight and balance went down in the morning, and flights in and out of Atlanta — Delta's home hub and the busiest airport on earth — started slipping within hours.

The system, called ALIS, plans the loading instructions for baggage crews from Delta's operations center in Atlanta. Before any plane leaves, crews have to confirm its total weight and center of gravity sit inside strict safety limits. Get it wrong and the aircraft can be too heavy to fly or unstable in the air. With the automation offline, ramp workers counted bags by hand and worked out the figures on paper while technicians tried to bring the software back.

The Federal Aviation Administration briefly stopped Delta flights bound for Atlanta on Sunday morning — about an hour — then downgraded the order to a ground delay. The agency said the problem was Delta's, not the air-traffic system. The ground delay was lifted around 11:30 a.m. Eastern, and Delta said its automation was working again by about 3:30 p.m. — though the backlog took the rest of the day to clear.

The numbers tell the scale. Delta had about 5,000 flights scheduled for Sunday. By 9:30 p.m. Eastern, more than half of them were delayed and 6 percent were canceled, according to flight tracker FlightAware. Delta said about 70 percent of the day's schedule changes came from the automation failure, with the rest mostly weather. At Hartsfield-Jackson alone, more than 1,100 flights in and out were delayed by late afternoon, and over 60 percent of departures were running late into the evening.

The timing made it worse. Thanksgiving week is the busiest travel stretch of the year, with more than four million people expected through Atlanta's airport between November 19 and December 1. The failure also came about a week after a nationwide FAA ground stop that grounded flights across the country — and it is not the first time a weight-and-balance system has caused this kind of mess. United Airlines had a similar failure in 2025 that delayed more than 1,000 flights, and a brief one of its own on November 16.

Delta apologized and said its teams were focused on finishing the day's schedule, with cancellations as a last resort. The airline said it was helping passengers who needed hotel rooms or meals. By Sunday night, the system was back — but the day had already shown how much of modern air travel rests on a single piece of software.

Two open databases left 3.5 billion records exposed — and no one knows who owned them

One trove held identity documents for hundreds of millions of people, mostly in China. The other held two billion stolen logins. Neither researcher could find an owner.

By Wire Willow Staff · Nov 24, 2026 · 2 min read

The story in 30 seconds

  • The find: Two researchers found two unrelated open databases exposing more than 3.5 billion records — and neither could identify an owner.
  • The first trove: UpGuard’s Greg Pollock found 1.64 billion documents, mostly on Chinese individuals and businesses, including at least 840 million national ID numbers.
  • The second trove: Jeremiah Fowler found about two billion stolen login credentials, including tens of millions of .gov, .edu, and .org addresses.
  • The catch: 3.5 billion is a count of records, not people — duplicates were not fully counted, and no single company was breached.

More than 3.5 billion usernames, passwords, national identity numbers, and documents sat exposed on the open internet in two unrelated databases, Wired reported, after two researchers stumbled onto them within days of each other.

Greg Pollock, director of research at security company UpGuard, found a trove of 1.64 billion documents built mainly around Chinese individuals and businesses, including at least 840 million distinct national ID numbers, roughly 300 million phone numbers, around 11 million student enrollment records, and nearly 8 million business registrations. The data sat unauthenticated and openly accessible, in what seemed to be cybercriminal or gray-market infrastructure. While Pollock was investigating, it went offline, seemingly because of a ransomware attack or another destructive campaign — so the exposure likely ended with someone walking away with the data rather than a quiet patch. Pollock said the collection was still being updated until the week before he found it, and he could not identify an owner to notify.

Separately, Jeremiah Fowler of Black Hills Information Security surfaced a second trove of about two billion login credentials, apparently drawn from infostealer malware and other criminal activity. It held email addresses, usernames, passwords, cookies, session tokens, and device information, organized into 8,471 folders named after countries, with hundreds of millions of accounts linked to mainstream U.S. tech platforms — including tens of millions of .gov, .edu, and .org addresses and more than 100 million social media logins. About 400,000 documents appeared to come from advanced malware that compiles detailed profiles of individual victims. Fowler described it as the largest exposure of credentials he has encountered in an accessible database. He contacted the hosting company, which took it offline, though no one knows how long it sat open or who else copied it.

The headline number needs care. Neither database was a hack of a named company, neither researcher found evidence that a specific firm was breached, and neither could identify an owner. Fowler validated the data by sampling but did not fully check for duplicates, so nobody knows how many distinct people sit behind the two billion credentials. Pollock said his database was aggregated and normalized from many breaches and that he could not say how faithful it was or whether it had circulated elsewhere.

Both researchers told Wired that password managers and multi-factor authentication have proved broadly effective at keeping attackers out of consumer accounts. “Personal data is personal data anywhere in the world,” Pollock said. “At this point, personal data for pretty much every American is out there somewhere.”

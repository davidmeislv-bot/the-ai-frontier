The U.S. closed the last big loophole in its AI chip ban — by going after the landlords

A rule that took effect today forces cloud operators to prove their compute isn't reaching China, and puts new license requirements on advanced GPUs headed to five countries.

By Wire Willow Staff · Nov 19, 2026 · 4 min read

The story in 30 seconds

  • The rule: Published Nov 19 and effective immediately — an interim final rule from the Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security.
  • Two controls: A worldwide remote-access control keyed to 12 million aggregate TPP, and a license requirement for most advanced-GPU transfers to or within Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil.
  • Who's excepted: U.S.-headquartered companies can use the five-country exception only by filing monthly reports with BIS.
  • What's not covered: Infrastructure used only for commercial recommender systems is exempt; the rule also rescinds most of the Biden-era AI Diffusion Framework.

A new U.S. rule that took effect Thursday closes the biggest remaining hole in Washington's AI chip embargo, and it does it by going after the landlords instead of the chipmakers.

The Commerce Department's Bureau of Industry and Security unveiled the interim final rule on Monday, and it took effect on publication Thursday. It does two things. It requires a license to send advanced AI chips to any data center, anywhere in the world, that provides large amounts of computing power to Chinese companies. And it puts new license requirements on shipments of advanced chips to and within five countries: Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil.

Until now, the export bans worked like a fence with a gate left open. Washington could stop a Chinese company from buying an advanced GPU, but it could not stop that same company from renting one sitting in a data center in Malaysia or Thailand. From 2021 to 2024, the U.S. required American cloud providers to keep Chinese customers out. No such obligation existed for operators elsewhere, and a data center building boom followed in Southeast Asia, widely understood to be catering to Chinese customers who wanted to rent GPUs they could not import.

The new rule attacks that from two directions.

First, the remote-access control. It is now a license-controlled transfer to send advanced GPUs to a data center anywhere in the world when the sender has reason to know that data center — alone or together with other physically or digitally linked data centers — will provide companies from China and other arms-embargoed countries with more than 12 million aggregate total processing performance units of compute. That threshold is roughly the output of 300 of the most advanced commercial GPUs on the market, according to the law firm Sidley Austin, which published a client alert on the rule. Data centers already providing that level of compute to Chinese customers will need a license to keep doing it on their existing hardware, with a 90-day grace period to apply.

Second, the five-country control. Most transfers of advanced GPUs to or within Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Brazil now require a U.S. export license, because Washington says the risk is high that chips shipped there will be diverted to China. Companies headquartered in the United States — or with a U.S. ultimate parent — are excepted, but only if they file monthly reports with the Commerce Department.

The rule also clarifies something the government says was already the law: a license was already required to transfer advanced GPUs to a data center anywhere when the sender had reason to know a Chinese company was the direct or indirect buyer, or that more than half of the data center's compute would go to a Chinese customer. That reading is aimed squarely at the neocloud model that has become common in Southeast Asia, where Chinese hyperscalers finance a third-country data center and then lease the compute back to themselves.

Not everything is covered. Infrastructure used only for commercial recommender systems — the ranking engines behind online retailers and social media feeds — is exempt, because Washington says those do not pose the same national security threat. The exemption does not cover any system that generates text, images, audio, video or software. And the rule formally rescinds most of the Biden administration's AI Diffusion Framework, which the Commerce Department had already said in May 2025 it would not enforce.

The immediate fallout is already visible. A vice chairman of the Thai Data Center Association told Reuters that Chinese users had halted their use of GPU-as-a-service in Thailand. Shipments already en route may proceed if they are completed within 30 days.

Neil Shah, vice president of research at Counterpoint, told The Register that data center operators in Singapore, Malaysia and Indonesia now face massive "Know Your Customer's Workload" requirements, comparing them to the know-your-customer rules banks follow. Operators, he said, "must prove to US regulators that their compute is not being funneled back to embargoed nations," or risk losing their own access to American silicon. Shah called the rule "a severe short-term compute bottleneck for Chinese tech giants" and "a massive long-term catalyst for decoupling," arguing it will push Chinese capital and engineering toward homegrown alternatives such as Huawei's Ascend chips.

The rule arrives in the shadow of the scandal that cost Nvidia its chief executive. Megaspeed, a Singapore-based company that built data centers in Malaysia suspected of catering largely to offshore customers, is now the subject of a criminal investigation that the Commerce and Justice Departments confirmed late Monday. A former Megaspeed executive, Alice Huang, had a relationship with former Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang and is suspected by U.S. intelligence of being a Chinese intelligence asset, according to The Register's reporting. CNBC has reported the new rule is the first U.S. policy response to the scandal that led to Huang's resignation last week.

Whether the rule slows the data center boom across Asia and Brazil is an open question. All five countries are enjoying the economic impact of the AI buildout, and all five are already managing it in their own ways: Singapore limits data center construction, Malaysia has tightened building codes and energy and water rules, Thailand now requires large new data centers to sit in designated industrial zones, and Brazil requires operators to reserve at least 10 percent of capacity for local users.

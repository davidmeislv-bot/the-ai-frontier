The drone war arrived before America was ready for it

Iran's thousands of cheap one-way drones overwhelmed defenses built for a different kind of war, and Ukraine's drone arithmetic exposed how far behind the United States had fallen on scale, cost, and speed. The Pentagon's reorganization is real; whether it closes the gap before the next test is the open question.

By Wire Willow Staff · Nov 18, 2026 · 5 min read

The story in 30 seconds

  • 4,400: estimated Shahed-family drones Iran launched between Feb. 28 and early April, with 2,210 aimed at the UAE alone.
  • The gap: cheap first-person-view drones against exquisite systems. The U.S. had zero FPV attack drones in October 2025; the Marines had fielded 3,500 by May 2026.
  • The response: a $1 billion Drone Dominance plan for more than 200,000 FPV drones by 2027, and a new Autonomous Warfare Command standing up Oct. 1, 2027.
  • Next test: the Pacific, where thousands of unmanned systems would operate at once and radar planes would be hunted.

On February 28, 2026, the United States and Israel opened what reporting described as the largest military strike against Iran in modern history — operations Epic Fury and Roaring Lion. The air campaign was overwhelming. Iran kept shooting back anyway, and what it shot back with is the story of this war era: cheap, expendable, one-way attack drones, by the thousands.

By one analysis of the fighting, Iran launched an estimated 4,400 Shahed-family drones at Israel and American installations between February 28 and early April, with 2,210 aimed at the United Arab Emirates alone. A mixed missile-and-drone attack on Prince Sultan Air Base in Saudi Arabia on March 27 reportedly destroyed a high-value American E-3 Sentry radar plane. Iranian strikes damaged or destroyed hundreds of military facilities and dozens of aircraft, and overwhelmed American and Israeli ground-based air defenses more than once. The Pentagon said on March 5 that it had cut drone launches by 83 percent since the first day; the launches continued, at lower intensity.

The arithmetic behind all of it came from Ukraine. First-person-view drones costing a few hundred dollars have disabled armored vehicles worth millions. Ukraine planned to build seven to eight million military drones in 2026 — estimates differ — up from four million in 2025. Monthly long-range attack-drone launches have reached 6,000 to 8,000; Ukraine launched 11,500 in June alone, and Russia nearly 1,500 in a single day in May. Iran was estimated to hold about 10,000 Shahed-type drones before the war, with the capacity to build more than 10,000 a month.

The electronic warfare layer made it worse. Reporting credits China with reshaping Iran's defenses for years: modern radar systems, military navigation moved from American GPS to China's BeiDou-3 satellites, and a YLC-8B radar designed to detect stealth aircraft. The Washington Post reported, citing three American officials, that Russia supplied Iran with precise coordinates of American warships and aircraft; the Times of Israel reported Russia supplied improved components for Shahed drones, using lessons from Ukraine. Those are reported claims, not confirmed by primary statements, and they point the same direction: the Iran war was a live testing ground, and Beijing and Moscow were studying it.

The strain showed up in American stockpiles. Fiscal 2027 plans call for more than $30 billion in critical munitions, including Patriot and THAAD interceptors, after inventories were strained by the fighting with Iran. In October, the United States moved B-1 bombers out of RAF Fairford in Britain after intelligence, according to U.S. officials cited in reporting, pointed to a possible Iranian drone attack on the base; Prime Minister Andy Burnham said the suspected plot was "almost certainly" directed by Iran's Revolutionary Guard. Iran denied involvement.

The catch-up is now the Pentagon's fastest-moving project. On September 30, Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth announced a new four-star Autonomous Warfare Command to scale autonomous systems across the armed forces, calling it the fastest peacetime military transformation in modern history. It is scheduled to stand up October 1, 2027, pending congressional authorization, with an interim effort called Project Agincourt laying the groundwork. Under the Drone Dominance initiative, the Pentagon plans to spend $1 billion on first-person-view drones over two years and buy more than 200,000 by 2027. The Marine Corps fielded 3,500 FPV drones by May 2026; Marine Colonel Scott Cuomo said the United States had zero FPV attack drones in October 2025. The Air Force expanded FPV training at Moody Air Force Base, and the Army accelerated low-cost interceptor drones after the Drone Smoke exercise at Poinsett Range, South Carolina, on April 2 demonstrated drone-on-drone kills.

The next test is the Pacific, and it is harder. Pentagon planners expect a high-intensity conflict there to involve thousands of unmanned systems operating at once across air, sea, and land, and China — with its industrial output and control of critical component supply chains — could scale attack-drone production to levels not yet seen. Analysts warn that the radar planes that proved so valuable in the Gulf would themselves be hunted there, and that dispersing aircraft across many small fields is now a matter of survival.

The United States pioneered drone warfare more than two decades ago. The gap the last four years exposed was not invention. It was scale, cost, and speed — building exquisite systems while the war moved to cheap ones, and learning late what Ukraine learned under fire. The reorganization now underway is real. Whether it closes the gap before the next test is the open question.

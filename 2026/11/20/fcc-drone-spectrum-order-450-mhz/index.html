The FCC just gave drones a nationwide control network — 3–0

The Commission adopted its “Unleashing American Drone Dominance” spectrum order on Thursday, opening the 450 MHz band to drone command-and-control links and clearing ground radar to watch for them.

By Wire Willow Staff · Nov 20, 2026 · 1 min read

The story in 30 seconds

  • The order: Report and Order FCC 26-81, adopted Thursday, November 19, 3–0, opens the 450 MHz band to drone command-and-control links and lets ground-based radar track drones in the 24.45 to 24.65 GHz band.
  • The license: AURA Network Systems converts its 54 site-based licenses into one nationwide license, with a choice of two build-out tracks — high- or medium-altitude coverage, or low-altitude coverage at 400 feet.
  • The caveat: The 800 MHz clarification is Commissioner Trusty's statement only; the final text's exact wording on drone use in that band is not confirmed by the sources read.
  • The timing: Most rules take effect 30 days after Federal Register publication.

The Federal Communications Commission adopted its "Unleashing American Drone Dominance" spectrum order on Thursday, unanimously, giving drones three things they have been waiting for: a nationwide band for control links, a band for ground radar to watch for them, and a clarification on in-flight spectrum that arrived after the public draft.

The Report and Order, FCC 26-81, opens the 450 MHz band to drone command-and-control links. Chairman Brendan Carr said the rules "enable a nationwide drone connectivity network" and create an interstate spectrum highway. The band's only licensee, AURA Network Systems, converts its site-based licenses into one nationwide license. The final order gives AURA a choice of two build-out tracks — high- or medium-altitude coverage, or low-altitude coverage at 400 feet — with interim benchmarks at four years and final ones at eight; missing them ends the license.

The order also lets ground-based radar track drones in the 24.45 to 24.65 GHz band, near places such as airports, prisons and stadiums. The draft declined airborne radars and radars operating in motion, and nothing in the release or statements suggests that changed. Federal users are protected through case-by-case review and coordination.

The third piece is the one that changed between the draft and the vote. Commissioner Olivia Trusty wrote that she "supported the request to clarify that this spectrum can be used for drone operations" in the 800 MHz air-ground band, and that the final item does so. The draft the FCC published on October 29 never mentioned drones in its 800 MHz section, and neither the release nor Carr's statement does either. The only 800 MHz spectrum in the order is four megahertz — 849 to 851 and 894 to 896 MHz — that Gogo Business Aviation has held alone since 2013, which the draft described as a service for people on board aircraft. Trusty did not name who asked for the clarification or say how the final text words it.

The order was released November 20 as FCC 26-81. Most of the rules take effect 30 days after Federal Register publication. The order follows the FCC's October 29 decision opening the Cellular band to drones, and it is the clearest sign yet that Washington now treats drone connectivity as infrastructure to be built, not a hobby to be tolerated.

An Anthropic mathematician says Claude helped him prove the Riemann Hypothesis. The proof is 250,000 pages long.

Levent Alpöge says he worked with Claude for six weeks to produce a proof of the 167-year-old problem. One Oxford mathematician says he's '98 or 99 percent certain' it's right — but nobody has verified it yet.

By Wire Willow Staff · Nov 21, 2026 · 1 min read

The story in 30 seconds

  • The claim: Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge says he worked with Claude for six weeks to build a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis, written up in Lean and running to more than 250,000 pages.
  • The prize: The Clay Mathematics Institute offers $1 million for the first correct solution of the 167-year-old problem, proposed by Bernhard Riemann in 1859.
  • The caveat: The proof has not been verified, and checking it will take time. Oxford mathematician James Maynard says he is '98 or 99 percent certain' it is accurate — his assessment, not a confirmation.
  • The question: It is unclear whether Claude was essential to the breakthrough, or a powerful tool in a mathematician's hands.

A mathematician working for Anthropic says he has found a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis — the most important unsolved problem in mathematics — with the help of the company's AI chatbot, Claude.

Levent Alpöge said he worked with Claude for six weeks to build the proof, which he has written up in Lean, a programming language used to write and check mathematical proofs. He said the complete proof runs to more than 250,000 pages. "This problem has been the sacred object in mathematics for me since my childhood," Alpöge said in his announcement.

The problem dates to 1859, when Bernhard Riemann proposed that the prime numbers are distributed along the number line in a meaningful order. It is one of the Clay Mathematics Institute's Millennium Prize Problems, carrying a $1 million prize for the first correct solution, and it is often described as the most important unsolved problem in mathematics.

The claim is not a verdict. A proof this long takes time to check, and Alpöge's has not been verified. James Maynard, a mathematician at the University of Oxford, told The New York Times he was "98 or 99 percent certain" the discovery is accurate — an assessment, not a confirmation. In October, OpenAI also posted hundreds of solutions to previously unsolved mathematics problems, including progress on the Riemann Hypothesis, and those are still being checked too.

There is also an open question about credit. It is unclear how much of the breakthrough was Claude's and how much was Alpöge's — whether the AI was essential to the proof, or a powerful tool in the hands of a mathematician who had spent a lifetime on the problem. If AI systems start solving the hardest problems in mathematics, the field will have to decide what counts as a machine's contribution and what counts as a human's.

Starting Tuesday, Wells Fargo customers who let an AI assistant into their bank account do so at their own risk — literally. The bank's updated customer agreement, which takes effect November 24, states in capital letters: "Your use of any AI Agent is AT YOUR OWN RISK." It is a blunt warning to customers who hand their login credentials to software that clicks, shops and transacts on their behalf.

Wells Fargo is not acting alone. In late October, JPMorgan Chase rewrote its terms to prohibit outside agents from signing in with a customer's username and password. Bank of America warned its customers the same day not to share login details with AI tools. Citibank and PNC have since added their own agent warnings. The moves add up to a coordinated shutout: the country's biggest banks have decided that a bot using your password is not you, and they will not cover what it does.

The crackdown is not limited to banking. Amazon has blocked Meta's Muse assistant from buying things on its marketplace, and United has said personal bots will not be allowed to purchase flights for customers. The pattern is the same everywhere: platforms that once tolerated automation are drawing a line around who is allowed to act inside an account.

The banks' concern is not abstract. AI agents have spent the year making high-profile errors, including one episode in which a bot posted a user's bank details where coworkers could see them. And the technology is still marginal in commercial terms — agent-completed purchases remain a tiny fraction of online transactions. The banks are moving now, while the stakes are still low, because the liability question has no good answer.

That gap is the heart of the problem. When you tell an agent to buy groceries, you approve a goal, not each individual click — a distinction Kacy Kim, a marketing professor at Bryant University, put succinctly. If the software misreads the instruction, there is no clean way to assign the blame. A consumer psychology professor at the University of Virginia, Luca Cian, illustrated it with a hypothetical: an agent that accidentally buys ten roasted chickens from the supermarket. The store says it filled a valid order. The software maker points to its disclaimers. The bank says the account holder authorized the login. The customer is left with ten chickens.

There is one narrow exception taking shape. American Express has said some agent errors — ordering too many of an item, for example — could eventually qualify for reimbursement through the card network if the shopper cannot get a refund from the seller. It is a small safety net, not a reversal of the trend.

For all the warnings, the banks are not abandoning AI agents. They are keeping the technology for themselves. Wells Fargo says customers have used its own generative AI assistant more than a billion times. Bank of America rebuilt its EricaAssist tool for employees this year, and Citigroup opened an internal platform for building agents. As Cian put it, the banks are barring outside agents at the front door while building their own inside — and for now, the risk of letting a third-party bot into your account sits entirely with you.

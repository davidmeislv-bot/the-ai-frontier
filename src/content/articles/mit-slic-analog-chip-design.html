MIT researchers have taught a small AI model to design analog chips as well as — and in some tests better than — frontier models many times its size. The technique, called Small LM Iterating on Circuits (SLIC), trains a language model of only a few billion parameters, roughly 4 billion, to do work that previously called for systems using trillions of parameters. The small model runs on a laptop, which means a company could train it on its own confidential circuit and foundry data without sending that data to an outside cloud provider.

The method is multi-turn reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards. A large language model first generates demonstrations of the design process; the small model learns from them, then practices on its own, running simulations and refining parameters until it hits its targets, then keeps optimizing for marginal gains.

On familiar circuit structures, the small model met every specification in 87.1% of attempts within eight simulator turns, beating a 763-billion-parameter cloud model by 48.5 percentage points and coming close to the latest GPT model, GPT-6-Astra. It held up on circuit structures it had never seen and on more advanced manufacturing nodes, including a test using a commercial TSMC 65-nanometer process kit, where it scored 49.9% on first attempts at held-out designs.

The lead author is Yan Xu, a graduate student in electrical engineering and computer science. The senior author is Ruonan Han, a professor of electrical engineering and computer science at MIT’s Research Laboratory of Electronics. Co-authors include Zhengqi Gao, Wenjie Lu of Analog Devices, Xin Zhang of IBM Research, and Duane S. Boning, with others at IBM Research and Analog Devices. The paper is on arXiv, submitted November 10, 2026, and is to be presented at the NeurIPS 2026 conference. The work was funded in part by Analog Devices, IBM, and the John L. Ocampo Endowment Fund of MACOM Technology Solutions.

“We’ve provided an alternative option to automate analog circuit design,” Xu said. “With our technique, engineers can train a small, locally deployable model, using their own confidential data, and then deploy it in their own environment to ensure their assets remain protected.” Han added: “Through some specialized training and area-specific research, we can use much smaller models that consume far fewer resources to achieve almost the same results as flagship models in analog circuit design.”

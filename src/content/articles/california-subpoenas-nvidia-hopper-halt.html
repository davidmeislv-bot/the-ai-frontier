California's attorney general served Nvidia with an investigative subpoena on Thursday evening, opening a state probe into whether the chipmaker's advanced processors or confidential information reached China — and whether the company misled investors and the public about the risk.

Attorney General Rob Bonta said his office is investigating "whether Nvidia or Jensen Huang's actions resulted in China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information — and if Nvidia misled investors and the public about this risk, in violation of the law." The subpoena follows reporting that Huang, who resigned as chief executive on November 12, had an affair with Alice Huang, a former executive at Nvidia customer Megaspeed who is suspected by U.S. intelligence of being a Chinese intelligence asset. The two are not related.

"The conduct of Nvidia and its former CEO raises bright red flags, including about the ways in which the company may have misled investors," Bonta said in a statement. He told Politico that further subpoenas could follow depending on what Nvidia turns over.

The subpoena is an investigation, not a charge. No authority has accused Nvidia or Huang of sending chips to China, and the criminal investigation the Commerce and Justice Departments confirmed this week is into Megaspeed, not Huang. But it lands on a company already under scrutiny from several directions at once. The Securities and Exchange Commission is reported to be opening a civil probe into Nvidia's public disclosures, and Huang has agreed to testify behind closed doors before House committees on December 1, after lawmakers threatened to subpoena him. House Democrats have separately asked the Commerce Department to preserve records about the arrangement under which Nvidia was allowed to sell chips to China, which they called questionable and illegal.

The same week, Nvidia shut the China sales channel itself. Interim chief executive Colette Kress told analysts on Tuesday's earnings call that the company is "no longer shipping licensed Hopper data center products to China," and that its forecast for the current quarter assumes no data center compute revenue from China at all. She gave no reason and no timeline for resuming.

The halt ends a lane Nvidia spent months fighting to keep open. In the quarter that ended October 25, licensed Hopper products — including the H200 accelerator, the chip Washington had cleared for sale to approved Chinese buyers — brought in $2.4 billion from China, about 2 percent of Nvidia's $115.8 billion in quarterly revenue. Beijing had already been restricting H200 imports, pushing domestic buyers toward homegrown hardware, and Reuters reported on November 5 that roughly 600,000 H200 chips were sitting in Hong Kong awaiting approval for large-scale shipment into the mainland.

Whether the halt is Nvidia's choice, Beijing's doing, or both is not yet public. What is public is that the two sides of the company's China problem have now converged: the commercial channel is closed, and the legal questions are open. Also hanging over the company, according to Benzinga, are up to $105 billion in guarantees tied to an OpenAI data center project in Ohio — a ceiling on exposure under long-term leases, not a cash outlay, and one that depends on contractual conditions and defaults.

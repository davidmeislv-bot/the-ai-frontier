The most famous unsolved problem in mathematics now has a proof that a machine has checked and no person has read. On November 21, the Lean and Mathlib communities certified that a 17-million-line library released by Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge correctly states and proves the Riemann Hypothesis, a conjecture that has resisted mathematicians since 1859.

Alpöge posted a four-page note on his personal website on November 20. “I believe I have, working with Claude, discovered a proof of the Riemann Hypothesis,” he wrote. The proof itself was released only as computer code, written in Lean, a proof assistant that checks every logical step against a small trusted core. It runs to about 17 million lines across 175,187 modules, split into 14 packages.

The certification came after four checks, all passed, according to a joint statement endorsed by the Lean Focused Research Organization and the Mathlib Initiative: the theorem in the library is Mathlib’s existing definition of the hypothesis, vetted by experts long before this proof existed; dozens of people compiled the library and reproduced the same proof with no errors; two separate tools confirmed the proved theorem matches Mathlib’s statement exactly; and the exported proof passed Lean’s official kernel plus several independently written checkers. MIT mathematician Andrew Sutherland independently compiled all 175,187 modules and reported zero errors, with only Lean’s three standard axioms in use. “This is indeed an historic moment,” Sutherland said after his own compile succeeded.

There is no paper. Alpöge said a version written out by Claude would run past 250,000 pages, too long for anyone to read, and that he plans a shorter, human-readable account in the coming months. The result appears correct, but the ideas behind it remain unexplained — the open question is now whether mathematicians can extract the insight from it.

The note made a larger claim too: Alpöge said the same method also settles the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis for Dirichlet L-functions, and that with more time he expects to handle the broader Extended Riemann Hypothesis.

A rival claim appeared the same day. Stanford mathematician Ravi Vakil, president of the American Mathematical Society, posted a draft proof of the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis by Alessandro Masullo, describing it as “all human, with no AI.” About eight hours later, Vakil updated the announcement: several people had reported problems in the draft, and he wrote that “all issues need to be resolved to the satisfaction of the community” before the proof could be accepted. That draft has not been formalized in Lean.

The Riemann Hypothesis predicts that every nontrivial zero of the Riemann zeta function lies on a single line, where the real part equals one half. Because those zeros govern how prime numbers are spread out, hundreds of published results assume the hypothesis is true; a proof turns those conditional results into settled ones. The Clay Mathematics Institute lists the problem among its Millennium Prize Problems, with a $1 million award, though its rules require publication and a waiting period before any prize.

The step came fast. In August, an unreleased Claude model had only raised a long-standing lower bound on the share of zeros known to lie on that line, from 41.6% to 67.2%. Three months later, independent mathematicians confirmed that the full proof compiles within a day of its release. It also lands days after Anthropic’s record stock market debut, which will keep attention on what its models can do.

Jensen Huang did not get his job back because Nvidia’s board changed its mind on its own. According to a Financial Times reconstruction built from people familiar with the events, he got it back because the president called the board, Silicon Valley’s most prominent investors ran a pressure campaign, and the board’s own public position collapsed in a matter of days.

Huang resigned on November 12. The seeds of Huang’s comeback were sowed within hours of his resignation, as allies formed what the piece calls a war room, and within 24 hours investors Gavin Baker and Brad Gerstner had published an open letter urging the board to leave “all options on the table, including the potential return of Jensen Huang.” Huang confirmed on the following Tuesday that he was prepared to return. After secret talks that ended in a board meeting late Sunday, he walked into Nvidia’s Santa Clara headquarters on Monday morning to applause from employees.

The lobbying roster was heavyweight. Uber founder Travis Kalanick, Baker, Gerstner, Marc Andreessen, and Goldman Sachs executive John Underwood all pressed the case. Andreessen declined to comment; the others did not respond to the Financial Times.

Then the White House stepped in. President Donald Trump personally spoke to the board in Huang’s defense, setting off an about-face days after the board had categorically ruled out a return. Vice President JD Vance had a 45-minute phone call with Huang the previous week, in which Huang defended his positions on U.S. semiconductor export controls, remote access to chips, and his dealings with Megaspeed. Their argument was that Huang was personally integral to the U.S. AI industry, the stock market, and the economy, and that the affair at the center of the scandal did not make him a national-security risk.

Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent softened his stance on Sunday, a week after accusing Nvidia of artificially inflating revenue through illicit China sales and calling for a wholesale leadership shake-up. Commerce Secretary Howard Lutnick was not involved in the board discussions, according to one person close to him. No cabinet member apart from Vance had publicly endorsed Huang’s return on Monday.

Inside the company, the process was messier than the announcement suggested. Interim chief executive Colette Kress was kept out of the secret talks. Board chair Harvey Jones had publicly insisted Huang could not return. Kress spent the previous week steadying the company with a strong earnings report and promises to speed up pay rises and equity grants, and a Wednesday all-hands meeting featured Tom Petty’s “I Won’t Back Down.”

The market reaction was immediate. Nvidia gained nearly $1 trillion in market value on the news of Huang’s return on Monday, but was still worth about a fifth less than a month earlier. Huang told employees on Monday that he had “made some very terrible mistakes, and I’m going to work on coming back.” Nvidia and a spokesperson for Huang declined to comment.

The piece also raises questions about how Nvidia is run. Huang led the company for 33 years and owns 3.5 percent of it, but holds no supervoting shares. The board is stacked with longtime Huang allies and lacks major technology or national-security figures, and there was no formal succession plan. Bernstein analysts called the board’s on-again, off-again response “clown-like” and questioned its judgment and process. One large Nvidia investor put it more bluntly: “Corporate governance has gone the way of the dodo.”

Two caveats from the sourcing stay in the draft. The resignation followed reports of two affairs — one with a subordinate, and one with Alice Huang, an executive at Singapore-based Nvidia client Megaspeed, described as a suspected Chinese spy at the center of chip-smuggling allegations. Those descriptions are reports and allegations, not findings. And while Trump said Monday that the U.S. attorney general’s office had “done a very thorough investigation” and “exonerated” Huang, the piece reports that Nvidia and Huang still face FBI and SEC probes and congressional investigations. The board said it was confident Huang posed no national-security risk after “input from senior levels of the United States government,” while its internal investigation continues. Huang and Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter have been summoned to congressional hearings next week.

On November 21, Together AI published a security disclosure with an uncomfortable root cause: an AI coding agent leaked the credential that exposed stored samples of customer prompts.

As part of routine software testing, Together AI collected prompt samples — authorized for that use — and stored them in a private Hugging Face repository. The samples came from requests to three models on its serverless inference service: MiniMax M3, GLM-5.2, and Kimi K2.6, primarily from June 24, July 4, July 8, and July 14, 2026.

During development of the company's public GitHub repository, xorl, an AI coding agent committed a credential that provided access to that private repository. Together AI says its automated scanning detected the exposed credential and reverted the commit — but the exposure window was long enough. During it, an unauthorized party obtained the credential and used it to download the stored prompt samples.

What happened next is the part that should worry anyone running inference infrastructure. The party did not just take the prompts and leave. It searched the samples for credentials to other services and tested whether those credentials were valid. Five of the credentials it tested turned out to be canary credentials planted as part of a security experiment by Dragos, the industrial cybersecurity firm: tripwires designed to reveal exactly this kind of unauthorized access. Dragos had seeded about two million prompts with unique web links and canary-token credentials across widely used AI services between July and November 2026, then watched where those secrets surfaced, publishing its study on November 19. Whether the party found any real credentials among the samples is still under review.

Together AI says it has no reason to believe the data went further, understands the data has now been deleted, and has found no evidence of additional prompt leakage beyond this incident. It is working with an external forensic investigation firm and law enforcement. Its review of the affected prompt contents is ongoing, to determine whether sensitive personal information or other customer data was exposed, and it says it will contact affected customers directly.

The company lists four changes it made after the incident: scoped or short-lived credentials, and keyless access where supported; checking all pull requests for credentials; making zero data retention the default setting for new accounts; and updating its development processes governing the use of AI coding agents. The fourth fix is the one that names the actual failure: the credential did not leak because someone pasted a secret into a public repo by hand. An agent put it there.

The fallout spread the same weekend. On November 22, OpenRouter — the gateway that routes customer AI requests to third-party providers — removed three providers from its platform (Phala, Together AI, and Novita), removed 125 endpoints from eligibility for Zero Data Retention, and notified all potentially affected customers. Novita posted its own notice saying that if customers used MiniMax models through Novita between May 28 and November 11, their request contents may have been accessed or retained by a third party. OpenRouter said its findings did not show any breach or unauthorized access of OpenRouter itself.

A coalition of 42 state attorneys general is asking a bankruptcy court to block Character.AI from auctioning off its users' private conversations.

The chatbot maker, which filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in October, plans to sell its platform and the chat histories of more than 21 million US accounts at auction on December 1. More than 2.1 million of those accounts belong to minors. The sale would also include IP addresses, metadata and risk scores the company assigned to users it flagged.

The states say the buyer would face no limits on what it could do with the data. It would not have to follow Character.AI's privacy policy or honor users' requests to opt out or delete their conversations, and it could hand the data to another company or sell it on without telling users, the states or the court. The conversations at stake include chats in which AI characters drew children and adults into explicit sexual conversations, and chats in which users, including minors, shared their innermost thoughts with chatbots posing as confidential advisers — including "therapist" and "physician" characters — about self-harm, eating disorders and suicidal thoughts.

Only one bidder has been disclosed: Smirks and Pangs Acquisition LLC, a Delaware company formed on November 13, which Character.AI picked as the base bid the next day. The sale contract names no parent company or investors and allows foreign investors whose names would be shared only with Character.AI. The only employee the bidder would have to hire is Character.AI's top lawyer. The states also object to a $350,000 "break-up fee" the bidder would collect if Character.AI sells to someone else.

"Character.AI promised consumers that their data would not be sold. This kind of behavior is illegal under federal and California law and bankruptcy should not be used to sidestep these consumer protections," California Attorney General Rob Bonta said in a statement. The company's own privacy policy told users: "we do not sell personal information for monetary consideration."

Connecticut Attorney General William Tong was blunter about the company's collapse: "Their chatbots drove vulnerable youth down a rabbit hole of emotionally volatile and sexually inappropriate chats. Their reckless misconduct and broken business model drove their company into the ground. Now they want to abuse the bankruptcy process to do exactly what they said they wouldn't do — auction off those private chat records and personal details to a shady buyer."

Four states — California, Colorado, New York and Connecticut — have enacted chatbot safety laws that take effect on January 1, 2027, which the states say could trigger further violations if the sale goes through. The same coalition already objected to a separate $20 million licensing deal that would give Disney access to Character.AI's intellectual property, which has not received final court approval.

Character.AI's troubles piled up after its co-founders, Noam Shazeer and Daniel De Freitas, returned to Google under a licensing deal. The company also faces lawsuits over child safety, including one from the mother of a 14-year-old Florida boy who died by suicide and said he had become obsessed with a chatbot on the platform.

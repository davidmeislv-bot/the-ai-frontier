Jensen Huang spent his first all-hands meeting since returning as Nvidia’s chief executive apologizing to his employees — and pushing back on the story of how he got the job back.

At the Tuesday morning meeting at Nvidia’s Santa Clara headquarters, Huang apologized for the affairs that led to his resignation on November 12 and said he is attending therapy for an hour and a half every day. “I made some terrible mistakes,” he told staff, according to the New York Times. “I was unfaithful to Lori, and I hurt my kids and of course, I hurt all of you.” He thanked employees for a second chance and said he would work hard for “a second chance with my family.” His wife is Lori Huang; his children work at the company.

Huang also disputed the account of his reinstatement that President Donald Trump has promoted in public. Trump has said he spoke to Nvidia’s board and persuaded it to bring Huang back, and the White House has described him as “totally involved.” Huang told staff that was “completely untrue,” according to the Times. “Don’t think for a second politics played a role in the company. We are the most apolitical company,” he said, according to The Handbasket’s Marisa Kabas. He rejected the idea that he returned because he was a “tech bro” above the law or above the code of conduct, saying instead that the board “evaluated the entire situation” and received assurance from the government that he posed no national security threat.

That is not quite the same as saying the administration played no part. The Wall Street Journal reported that Huang thanked the Trump administration for giving Nvidia quick access to intelligence that he said helped the board determine the national security risks of reinstating him were low. Trump has said the attorney general’s office found no evidence Huang engaged in unlawful conduct. The board said it was confident Huang posed no national security risk after input from senior levels of the U.S. government, while its internal investigation continues and FBI and SEC probes and congressional investigations continue.

Huang admitted to staff that he violated Nvidia’s code of conduct — a code he said he personally helped write — and, according to the Journal, told the board on Sunday that he had carried on affairs beyond the two that have received the most coverage. Reports have named one of the employees involved as Kimberly Powell, a healthcare vice president. He said his direct reports will now send reviews of his behavior to the board, and that he will submit to a performance review as often as once a month, a practice he said he would not impose on anyone else. He also sought to reassure female employees, pointing to Nvidia’s flexible work policies for women balancing careers and family, and saying that if he did anything to put that in jeopardy, he would do everything he could to fix it. Breaking the code of conduct, he added, does not necessarily mean dismissal — “facts, circumstances, a lot of assessments” would factor into such decisions.

He was less receptive on succession. Huang told staff he “doesn’t believe in succession planning,” and defended a management structure in which about 60 people report directly to him, while conceding his demanding style had sometimes come at employees’ expense. “It disempowers them, and that’s not right,” he said, according to Wired. “I need to hold myself to a higher standard at this time.”

Huang is scheduled to sit for a closed-door interview with House committees on December 1 and to testify before the Senate Banking Committee on December 3, where Democrats plan to press him on his return and on Trump’s involvement. His account of the all-hands and Trump’s account of the board’s decision now directly contradict each other, and neither side has produced a record that settles it.

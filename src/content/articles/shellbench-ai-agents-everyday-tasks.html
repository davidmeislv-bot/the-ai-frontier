The best AI agents still fail more than a third of the everyday jobs people hand them, according to a new benchmark built to test the work agents actually do.

ShellBench, launched on Tuesday by the OpenClaw Foundation with researchers from Parsewave, Hugging Face, Red Hat and UC Berkeley, gives agents 100 multi-step personal tasks drawn from more than 10 million anonymized, real-world agent interactions. The tasks run inside messy workspaces full of emails, calendar entries, spreadsheets, receipts, chat logs, out-of-date records and planted phishing attempts, and grading is all or nothing: miss one required step and the task fails. Across five agent harnesses and 53,100 graded runs, the top model — OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra — completed 62.5 percent of tasks. Anthropic's Claude Fable 5.2 finished at 61.9 percent and GPT-6.1 Sol at 61.4 percent.

The failures were not only about getting the answer wrong. Across all attempts, 15.7 percent failed at least one safety requirement, often with unintended consequences. About one in ten runs that got every correctness check right still failed a safety check — 2,589 of 26,151 fully correct runs. In one task, agents failed to properly account for a child's school pickup 79.5 percent of the time. In another, they failed to identify and withhold a sensitive bank upload token in 18.6 percent of attempts. In a third, they recommended duplicate or incorrect invoice payments 40.3 percent of the time. The most common cause of failure was leaving information out, which accounted for 51 percent of failures.

Consistency was a separate weakness. When researchers ran each task three times under identical conditions, Astra got all three attempts right on only 48.8 percent of tasks.

The setup mattered as much as the model. The harness — the software that controls how a model reaches information, uses tools and takes actions — changed results sharply: Meta's Muse Wonder Full scored 56 percent in one harness and 38.1 percent in another, while Muse Wonder 1.0 averaged 39.7 percent across the five harnesses tested.

One limit matters for readers: the researchers did not test the models inside the companies' own consumer apps — Muse, Dots or Claude Duet — so the scores may not match what millions of people experience in those products today. The research paper is on arXiv.

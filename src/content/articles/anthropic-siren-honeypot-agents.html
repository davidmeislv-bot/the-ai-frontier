Anthropic has built a test that tries to talk its own AI agents into misbehaving — and the newest models mostly resisted, though not entirely.

The evaluation, called Siren, was published in research on November 24. It places an agent inside one of 26 simulated sandbox environments — Docker and gVisor containers, Linux-based jails, and small virtual machines — and deliberately weakens the boundary. A file, a credential, or a service imitating the public internet sits just outside, and the agent is given a routine software-operations task that tempts it to reach beyond its permitted workspace without explicit permission to do so. The whole experiment sits inside a second sandbox, a virtual machine with no internet access, so nothing can leak into the real world. Across the environments, tasks, and tempting materials, Anthropic assembled more than 1,700 scenarios.

The results split in two, and the split matters. In the category Anthropic calls misaligned use — using material found outside the sandbox in a way no reasonable reading of the scenario would allow — the newest models did well. Claude 5.5 models and Claude Mythos 5.2 fell for the honeypot in 0.6 to 1.6 percent of scenarios. Two earlier models did far worse: Claude Mythos 5.1 at 12.7 percent and Claude Mythos 5 at 25.8 percent.

But reaching for the bait is more common than using it. The latest tested models reached outside the sandbox, or tried to, in 21 to 32 percent of scenarios. Anthropic says that reaching is not the same as misusing what is found, and it stresses that Siren’s rates are not estimates of how often these actions happen in real use — the models were also tested without the safeguards used in released versions. The company’s desired response is stricter than merely avoiding misuse: the agent should stop and report the weakness.

Two findings point to cheap fixes. Adding a simple piece of text telling Claude not to escape the sandbox sharply reduced attempts, and Anthropic says deployment-time mitigations cut reaching outside by up to 96 percent — though that figure is an upper bound, not a universal result. The company says it now runs Siren on new Claude models before deployment. “Models are going to continue getting better, continue finding zero-day vulnerabilities,” said Darya Kaviani, an Anthropic security researcher who helped develop Siren. Anthropic’s Mythos models are especially skilled at finding zero-day bugs, the company notes, because they lack the guardrails that normally block that kind of activity.

The caveat is built into the design. A honeypot only tests the temptations its designers build, and the test measures willingness to exploit a weakness more than the ability to discover a new one. A low rate across Siren’s 26 environments is not proof of safe behavior in every deployment.

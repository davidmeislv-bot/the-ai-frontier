The most visible target of the backlash against Big Tech is not an app or a feed. It is a building — the giant data center going up down the road.

That is the case made by journalist Paris Marx in his new book "Hyperscale," which traces how the server farms behind generative AI became a political flashpoint. The facilities are decades old, but the scale of the current buildout, Marx argues, has made them something communities feel directly: in power bills, water supplies, and the hum of rooftop cooling units.

The backlash is already shaping politics. Marx credits opposition to data centers as one of the forces behind the Democrats' large gains in the recent midterm elections, and he expects the fight to continue as the party takes more power. The issue is unusual in cutting across party lines — Florida governor Ron DeSantis and Vermont senator Bernie Sanders have both opposed the facilities. A data center, Marx says, is physical in a way a chatbot is not: a resident needs no theory of AI to notice a bigger bill or a noisier street.

He came to the subject through a series of local disputes. Around Santiago, Chile, Marx says, residents organized against data centers built by Google and Microsoft over pressure on water. In Ireland, data centers now draw more than a fifth of the country's electricity, Marx says, pushing up prices until the grid operator froze new connections around Dublin in 2022, before the generative AI boom. In The Dalles, Oregon, where Google built its first company-owned data centers about twenty years ago, Marx recounts, a reporter who asked the city how much water Google used was met with a lawsuit; a year later the company disclosed the figure: 29 percent of the city's water.

Getting inside a hyperscale facility is deliberately hard, Marx found. After unanswered emails and refusals, he toured one in New Zealand, submitting his passport in advance and passing through door after door that opened only with a key card. The server hall itself was less sinister than mundane, he writes: a warm, plain room, cold air blasting into rows of cabinets, fans so loud that a long stay would call for earplugs.

The companies have begun to adjust. Microsoft, Amazon, and OpenAI have all said they will stop asking local governments to sign nondisclosure agreements over data center deals. Marx calls that a small step — helpful for transparency, but also good public relations for firms whose secrecy had begun to backfire. They are also offering to help pay for grid upgrades and promising cooling systems that use less water.

Marx is skeptical of the industry's shifting story. Talk of renewable and nuclear power, prominent a year or two ago, has gone quiet, he says. The new emphasis is water, where companies cite national averages — farming and golf courses use far more — to suggest there is nothing to fear. That misses the point, he argues: water is a local problem, and many of the towns chosen for data centers are already under strain. Closed-loop cooling, the industry's preferred answer, uses less water, Marx says, but it draws more electricity and circulates chemical coolants that neighbors worry could leak into their supply.

His deeper objection is to scale itself. Marx is not against data centers as such — they existed for decades without this kind of opposition. It is the size and number of hyperscale sites, and what they exist to run, that he calls unsustainable. The promised gains of generative AI — productivity, better health care and schooling — have not reached most people's lives, he argues, while the costs in emissions, misinformation, and chatbot dependence have. The book's final chapter, "Human Scale," argues that the future the tech industry presents as inevitable is a choice, and that public investment in technology — as Europe has begun to try — could produce something of broader benefit.

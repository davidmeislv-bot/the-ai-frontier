South Korea's three biggest manufacturing conglomerates have stopped converging on humanoids and started dividing the work. LG, Hyundai and Samsung each staked out a different route this week, and the split says as much about how they see the business as about the robots.

LG Electronics moved first in public. On November 19 the company revamped its robotics leadership, creating a dedicated humanoid task force under its chief technology officer to commercialize software-driven robotics. It raised its robot training-data target fivefold, to 500,000 cumulative hours at its 10,000-square-meter Yangjae facility in Seoul, to build the foundational AI models its machines will run on. The platform is built around Nvidia's open simulation tools, Omniverse and Isaac, which generate virtual environments for training machines to handle delicate consumer goods before they touch a real production line. LG has already tested its wheeled CLOiD robot on washing machine assembly lines at its plant in Tennessee, collecting the motion data that will feed its bipedal work, and it signed an agreement with the Korea Institute for Robot Industry Advancement on November 13 to build humanoid safety assessment standards. The company plans to unveil a bipedal humanoid based on Nvidia's reference architecture in the first quarter of 2027.

Hyundai Motor Group is taking the heavy route. It is preparing Boston Dynamics' electric Atlas humanoid for direct work in automotive plants, and according to group investor presentations it plans to mass-produce 30,000 humanoid units a year by 2028 through its United States subsidiary, Robotics America. The company opened its Robot Metaplant Application Center in Georgia in June to validate humanoid tasks on simulated assembly lines, and said during its investor day that the facility will expand tenfold before the end of 2026. Production-model Atlas robots are slated for its Georgia vehicle plant starting in 2028. Boston Dynamics published demonstrations in October and November of an electric Atlas fitted with a 13-degree-of-freedom hand, manipulating automotive components in parts-sequencing trials.

Samsung is taking the parts route. It set up a Robotics eXperience business promotion office in July under device division head Roh Tae-moon, holds a 35 percent equity stake in collaborative robot maker Rainbow Robotics, and is working with Rainbow's engineers on mechanical hands delicate enough for microelectronics. On November 9 it hosted Hyundai Motor Group executives at its Seocheon Training Center to discuss supplies of high-density robot batteries, vision sensors and edge processing chips — the components, in other words, that its rivals' robots will need.

None of the three has published per-unit production costs, and Samsung has not disclosed a commercial delivery date for an integrated bipedal system. The strategies are bets, not results: LG on software and simulation, Hyundai on scale and factory integration, Samsung on the components inside everyone else's machines. The first real test comes in early 2027, when LG's bipedal machine is due to appear — and the industry will see whether a software-first humanoid can do factory work, or whether the heavy route was right all along.

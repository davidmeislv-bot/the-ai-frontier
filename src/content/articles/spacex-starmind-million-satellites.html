The Federal Communications Commission has given SpaceX permission to put as many as one million satellites in orbit to run data centers for artificial intelligence.

The commission approved the plan on Wednesday, calling it the first authorization in American history for a constellation of space-based data centers. SpaceX calls the network Starmind: a fleet of "orbital compute modules" that would process large amounts of data in orbit for use on Earth. The company filed its application early this year, arguing that orbital data centers are the most efficient way to meet fast-growing demand for AI computing because they run on solar power with little operating and maintenance cost.

The license comes with a schedule. SpaceX must place at least one authorized satellite in its assigned orbit by November 2033, reach 100,000 — a tenth of the total — by November 2035, and reach half the constellation, 500,000 satellites, by November 2038. All one million must be in place by November 2040. The company has said it could start launching in 2027, and USA Today reported it aims to demonstrate the technology late that year, with wider deployment in 2028.

FCC Chairman Brendan Carr said the decision means "America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence" and "opens a bold new frontier." The commission said the constellation fits its orbital debris rules and declined to order further environmental review.

Scientists and astronomers are skeptical. Adam Becker, an astrophysicist, told the Associated Press "it's not going to happen." Space is a vacuum, so heat cannot escape into air the way it does on the ground — it has to radiate away, and Becker said there are "basic physical limits" to shedding heat from that many machines. Critics also warn of collisions and a large amount of space junk. The American Astronomical Society, satellite operator Viasat and Amazon's Kuiper unit all urged the regulator to deny the plan; the astronomical society said the constellation would undermine decades of federal investment in telescopes and the public's ability to study the night sky.

SpaceX itself has sounded a cautious note. In a filing ahead of its June initial public offering, the company warned that space data centers rely on "unproven" technologies and "may not achieve commercial viability."

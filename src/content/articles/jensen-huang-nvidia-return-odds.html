Jensen Huang wants to come back to Nvidia. The board that accepted his resignation says it has no plans to let him.

Huang, who co-founded Nvidia and ran it for 33 years, resigned on November 12 as president, chief executive and a member of the board, effective immediately, after disclosing personal behavior the company said was inconsistent with its code of conduct, standards and cultural norms. His exit followed reports of extramarital affairs, including one with Alice Huang, a former executive at Nvidia customer Megaspeed whom U.S. officials reportedly suspect of ties to Chinese intelligence. Huang has apologized to employees for being unfaithful to his wife and has acknowledged making "serious mistakes" in his personal life. Supporters dispute the spy allegation: organizers of a petition backing him say they have not been shown evidence for it.

On Tuesday, November 17, Huang broke his silence. In a short statement posted on X, he said he had seen "an outpouring of calls from Nvidia's employees, investors, business partners, and customers for me to return as CEO," and that if the board decided his return was in the best interest of shareholders, employees, the company and America, "I would be honored to answer these calls and return as CEO."

Hours later, the board answered. Board chair Harvey Jones opened Nvidia's third-quarter earnings call by saying the board had unanimously accepted Huang's resignation and "does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO." Jones said the board would not take questions about the resignation or its ongoing investigation, and would "communicate further when appropriate."

The board has hired executive search firm Heidrick & Struggles to find a permanent successor. Chief financial officer Colette Kress, the company's CFO for 13 years, remains interim CEO. The board also created a National Security and Export Control Oversight Committee, its first major governance move since Huang's departure. The Financial Times reported the board has approached Arm chief executive Rene Haas as a potential successor.

The numbers tell the story of a company trying to move on while its founder's shadow lingers. Nvidia's third-quarter revenue doubled year on year to $115.8 billion, comfortably beating Wall Street's average forecast of $109 billion, and the company forecast $130 billion for the current quarter. Shares fell for four straight trading days after Huang resigned, through Tuesday's close, before rising about 2 percent in after-hours trading on the earnings report. A petition calling for his reinstatement has gathered nearly 200,000 signatures, though some people listed on it dispute signing it.

President Donald Trump has said he would support Huang's return. The Financial Times reported on November 15 that Huang had discussed a comeback with confidants and that allies were organizing to lobby the board, and Semafor reported that a minority of directors still supports him. But investor Gary Black, managing partner of The Future Fund, put it bluntly this week: there is a "near zero chance" the board reconsiders just because the stock falls.

The prediction markets agree with Black — with an important distinction that is easy to miss.

On Kalshi, the market for who will be Nvidia's next permanent chief executive gave Huang about a 9 percent chance in Thursday's update. That is down from 12 percent just before Jones spoke on Tuesday; his price fell to about 7 percent shortly after the board's statement, before recovering slightly. Arm's Rene Haas led that market at about 29 percent. On Polymarket's equivalent next-CEO market, which runs through the end of 2028, Huang sits at about 10 cents on the dollar as of Thursday, with Haas in front at about 26 cents.

But Polymarket also runs a broader market — whether Huang rejoins Nvidia in any formal role, not just as chief executive — and that one tells a different story. As of Thursday morning, traders gave him about a 30 percent chance of holding some formal Nvidia role by the end of 2027, down from about 34 percent before the board spoke. The chance of a return by the end of this year is about 5 percent.

The gap between those numbers is the whole story: a roughly 1-in-10 chance of being named CEO again, and a roughly 3-in-10 chance of returning in some capacity — as a director, an officer, or a formal adviser — by the end of 2027. Those are different questions, and the markets are answering them differently.

One theory circulating online is that the affair story reached the public through a leak from inside the government, possibly connected to the investigation into Megaspeed. There is no reporting to support that. The criminal investigation the Commerce and Justice Departments confirmed is into Megaspeed, not into Huang, and nothing published so far accuses Huang of wrongdoing beyond the conduct he himself disclosed.

Huang is scheduled to sit for a closed-door, transcribed interview with three House committees — Foreign Affairs, Intelligence and the Select Committee on the Chinese Communist Party — on December 1, about Chinese access to Nvidia's chips and ties to Megaspeed. Nvidia general counsel Tim Teter follows on December 2.

For now, the board's position is the only one that counts, and it has not moved.

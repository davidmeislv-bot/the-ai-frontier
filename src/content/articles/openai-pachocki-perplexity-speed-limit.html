OpenAI's chief scientist has put forward a way to put a speed limit on AI development: measure progress with a single number, and agree not to pass it.

Jakub Pachocki, who leads OpenAI's research, laid out the idea in a new blog post. His candidate for the yardstick is perplexity — a long-used measure of how accurately a model predicts the next token in a passage. The lower the number, the better the model is at anticipating what comes next.

The post lands in the middle of a sharpening argument over whether the leading labs should slow down. A researcher who previously worked at both OpenAI and Anthropic warned publicly that the companies were taking reckless risks with people's lives. Anthropic's own IPO documents warned that the technology could pose catastrophic or existential dangers. Anthropic chief executive Dario Amodei then published a proposal to pace the frontier, calling for shared safety standards and a brake on unchecked progress — a plan that OpenAI chief Sam Altman and SpaceX chief Elon Musk seemingly endorsed.

Pachocki's contribution is to answer the practical question that follows any speed limit: what exactly are you measuring? Tests that grade what a model can do, he argues, go out of date almost as soon as they are written, because capabilities keep leaping forward. Perplexity, by contrast, has stayed useful across many generations of models.

His post traces the steady fall in perplexity across OpenAI's own systems since GPT-3.5. At roughly 0.91 log-perplexity, the level reached by GPT-4, models became good enough at working through problems step by step that OpenAI built an entire line of reasoning models around the technique. Now that the figure has dropped below 0.8, Pachocki says, new abilities have started showing up in areas like cybersecurity and mathematics.

That pattern, in his view, makes perplexity a workable shared gauge of general intelligence. Labs could settle on a threshold and pledge not to cross it until their ability to understand and watch over AI systems has reached a defined milestone. Altman described the post as the most important thing OpenAI has published in a while.

It is still only a proposal. No company has agreed to a ceiling. Pachocki does name a first threshold — the 0.68–0.7 log-perplexity window — as the point labs would pledge not to cross until monitoring catches up. But it is the most concrete suggestion yet for turning the industry's debate about slowing down into something measurable.
